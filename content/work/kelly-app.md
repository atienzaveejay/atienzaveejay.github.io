## **Context**

Kelly was an early-stage prediction market app entering the Philippines, where users predict real-world outcomes (weather, traffic, events, trends) and earn rewards for correct predictions. It was designed as a **non-gambling alternative to betting platforms**.

### Constraints:

Monetization wasn’t possible yet, pending licensing. There was zero existing user base, no prior marketing system, and high skepticism. Many users perceived it as a scam outright. The goal wasn’t revenue yet. It was:

> **Acquire users early and build long-term adoption in a trust-sensitive category.**

---

## **Challenge: Selling a Concept People Don’t Understand**

We faced three core barriers.

### 1. Trust barrier

Being a finance-related product bred high skepticism on its own, and foreign founders added an extra layer of doubt, and users were hesitant to even try it.

### 2. Education barrier

“Prediction market” isn’t an intuitive term, and traditional content explained the concept instead of the behavior.

### 3. Audience problem

“Filipinos” was too broad a target, with no clear starting segment.

---

## **Insight: Don’t Teach the Product, Show It Through Culture**

The initial approach, run by an agency, leaned on educational content: definitions, explanations, technical breakdowns. It didn’t work.

### Shift:

Instead of explaining prediction markets…

> We embedded the product into **real-world events people already care about**

Traffic in Metro Manila, celebrity trends, news events, weather predictions. That’s what made the product feel familiar, relevant, and engaging, instead of foreign and abstract.

---

## **Strategy: Build a Content-Led Acquisition Engine**

---

### **1. Narrow the Audience (Critical decision)**

We focused on **college students**: early adopters, highly active on social media, with strong long-term retention potential.

> Win the user early, and you win them long-term.

---

### **2. Event-Based Content System**

Instead of asking “what is a prediction market?” we asked “what’s happening right now?” and built content around trending events, relatable scenarios, and culturally relevant topics.

---

### **3. High-Velocity Content Formats**

The top-performing format was the **1-minute news roundup video**: fast-paced, multiple topics in one reel, high information density. Our best-performing video hit **89,000 views**, with a consistent baseline of 1,000+ views per post.

> Topic selection matters more than format optimization.

---

### **4. Incentive Design to Drive Action**

Since monetization wasn’t allowed, we simulated value.

### Phase 1: Micro-incentives

Mobile load rewards (₱5 for a set number of correct predictions) mimicked real-world currency behavior and brought in ~5,000 users.

---

### Phase 2: High-stakes leaderboard

A monthly leaderboard competition, with an iPhone as the grand prize and vouchers or telecom credits as secondary rewards, supported by paid ads built around clear prize-driven messaging and simple participation mechanics.

---

## **Paid Strategy: CAC Optimization Through Relevance**

We ran Meta ads to scale acquisition. What worked was prize-led creatives with the iPhone as the anchor, event-based messaging instead of technical explanations, and a clear user action: predict, then win. CAC dropped from **₱20 → ₱12 (~40% drop)**.

> Better messaging and alignment, not lower spend.

---

## **Funnel: Awareness → Participation → Retention**

1. **Content**: event-based, high-engagement

1. **Ad**: incentive-driven (leaderboard, rewards)

1. **App**: prediction mechanics and reward simulation

---

## **Results**

- **23,000 users acquired in peak month**

- ~5,000 users from initial incentive system

- Social engagement:
  - 200K+ on Facebook
  - 84K+ on Instagram

- CAC reduced:
  - ₱20 → **₱12**

---

## **Key Learnings**

### 1. Content must match behavior, not logic

Educational content failed. Event-driven content worked.

---

### 2. Incentives must feel real

Mobile load worked because it’s “daily currency”. An abstract reward wouldn’t have landed the same way.

---

### 3. Retention matters as much as acquisition

Short-term monthly contests drove spikes but reduced long-term motivation. In hindsight, longer competitions with broader reward distribution would have served retention better.

---

## **Takeaway**

The agency before us tried to explain what a prediction market is. We stopped explaining it and started showing it inside things people already followed: Metro Manila traffic, celebrity news, the weather. That single switch is what moved the product from “is this a scam?” to 23,000 users acquired in a single month.

What made it repeatable:

- A single, narrow starting audience (college students) instead of “Filipinos”

- Incentives that mimicked real currency (mobile load) before we could offer real money

- Content built around “what’s happening now,” not “what is this product”

The one thing I’d change: our leaderboard contests were monthly and high-stakes, which spiked acquisition but didn’t hold retention. Longer contests with wider prize distribution would have kept more of those 23K users active past month one.
